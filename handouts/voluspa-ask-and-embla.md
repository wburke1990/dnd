# Völuspá 17–18 — Ask and Embla; and stanza 65

> **Real-world source text, kept verbatim.** *Völuspá* ("The Wise-Woman's
> Prophecy"), from the *Poetic Edda*, translated by **Henry Adams Bellows** (1923;
> the widely circulated reprint is dated 1936) — public domain. The Old Norse survives
> in the **Codex Regius** (c. 1270) and, in a shorter and partly different version, in
> **Hauksbók** (c. 1302–10).
>
> **In-world use:** the creation the Lonka zealots teach at the two trees on the
> Jotunheim ring-wall. See [Askr and Embla — the story the zealots supplied](../world/lonka/lore/askr-and-embla.md)
> and [Lonka / Jotunheim](../world/lonka/lore/lonka-jotunheim.md) §5.

---

## The making of the first pair — stanzas 17–18

```
Then from the throng | did three come forth,
From the home of the gods, | the mighty and gracious;
Two without fate | on the land they found,
Ask and Embla, | empty of might.

Soul they had not, | sense they had not,
Heat nor motion, | nor goodly hue;
Soul gave Othin, | sense gave Hönir,
Heat gave Lothur | and goodly hue.
```

Two things are worth noticing in the Norse itself. The poem does **not** say they are
trees — it says the three gods found two on the land, without fate and empty of might.
The trees come from Snorri, two hundred years later. And *Embla* is not securely "elm";
the reading is a scholarly reconstruction.

---

## Stanza 65 — the one that is not in both books

```
There comes on high, | all power to hold,
A mighty lord, | all lands he rules.
```

This stanza is **absent from the Codex Regius.** It survives only in Hauksbók, where
the leaf is barely legible. A ruler who comes from above and holds all power, arriving
after the world has ended, is the standard example of a Christian hand in a pagan poem.

**Two manuscripts of the same poem, and one of them has a stanza the other does not.**
That is the object to put in the party's hands.
