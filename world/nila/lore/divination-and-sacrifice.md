# Divination and Sacrifice across Nila

> **DM reference.** Every region on Nila takes omens, and no two do it the same
> way. This is the working catalogue: what is killed, what is read off it, what
> question it can answer, and how to run it at the table. All of it is real
> practice from the primary sources, used as-is — the campaign's standing method
> ([myth & poem as literal truth](myth-and-poem-as-literal-truth.md),
> [overview §3D](campaign-overview.md)). Legend: `[settled]` · `[loose]` ·
> `[OPEN]` · 🔒 DM secret.
>
> Kuru's system has its own file: [the Flame of Ankalia](../../kuru/lore/the-flame-of-ankalia.md).
> The sea's weather-omens have theirs:
> [saga weather omens](../../the-sea/lore/saga-weather-omens.md).

## 1. The three rules

`[settled]` **It works.** Divination on Nila is not a confidence trick and not a
spell. A reading done correctly gives the true answer. The campaign treats the
practice as plain fact, as it does elsewhere.

`[settled]` **It answers one narrow question, in its own vocabulary.** Each method
below can answer one kind of question and no other. A liver will tell you whether
the road is open; it will not tell you who is lying. The answer arrives in the
terms of the method — a quarter of sky, a side of the body, a crack running to an
edge — and the reader has to translate. Readers disagree.

`[settled]` **It costs a life.** Somebody's goat. An army on campaign kills its own
livestock to take omens, so a general who sacrifices before every engagement is
slaughtering his baggage train, and a long stalled siege runs out of beasts. When
the herd is gone, either the omens stop or the reader kills something else.

`[loose]` 🔒 **Why the organs carry an answer.** The beast grazed on the island. The
island is a god's stone in its hardened phase
([Kuru & the phoenix §2](../../kuru/lore/kuru-and-the-phoenix.md)). What the beast ate
passed through its liver. So the liver of a sheep raised on a god reports the sky
over that god, and a liver read in Maalm and a liver read on Mukka do not report the
same sky. Nobody states it this way. A party can work it out from the readings.

## 2. The liver

`[settled]` **Where:** Mukka, and wherever Mukka ships put in. Also Kalikhat's
surface towns and old Maalm.

The beast is thrown, the throat cut, the belly opened, and the liver lifted out
whole and still warm. The reader holds it in both hands and turns it.

**The liver is divided into sixteen parts, and each part is named for a god.** The
same sixteen as the sixteen quarters of the sky. A reader works with a bronze
model of a liver, life-size, with the sixteen fields scored into it and the names
cut inside them, and he sets the real organ beside the model to find his place.
Every reader carries one. They are heavy and expensive, and they get stolen.

What is read:

| Feature | Its name | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| The small pointed lobe | **the head** | Present and well-formed: proceed. **Absent: the thing you are asking about is already dead.** No second reading is taken. |
| The deep fissure | **the gate of the palace** | Doubled, there is a second claimant to whatever you hold. |
| The long groove | **the road** | Where it runs, your road runs. If it forks, both roads are open and neither is safe. |
| The gall bladder | **the bile** | Swollen: anger, and it is aimed at you. |
| Any mark on the left | — | Concerns the enemy. |
| Any mark on the right | — | Concerns you. |

`[settled]` **The missing head is the one everybody knows.** A reader who opens a
beast and finds no head-lobe puts the liver down and says so in front of whoever
paid for it. The camp knows when a general marches anyway.

**At the table.** A liver reading takes about two minutes and gives one answer in
the method's own terms. Use it when you want the party to know something and still
be free to ignore it. Mukka crews read a liver before a boarding, so any party
sailing a hired hull gets one whether they asked or not.

## 3. Birds

`[settled]` **Where:** Kuru (the corpse-towers — see
[the Flame of Ankalia §4](../../kuru/lore/the-flame-of-ankalia.md)), Brauron, and the
Kurunatukas.

### The marked-out sky

The reader takes a crooked staff, faces a fixed quarter, and **cuts a rectangle in
the air** — naming its corners aloud, left, right, near, far. From then until he
releases it, **only what crosses inside that frame counts.** A bird outside it does
not count. He sits down and does not turn his head.

Then the distinctions, which the readers will not let you blur:

- **Birds read by voice** — the raven, the crow, the owl, the hen. What matters is
  the cry and which side it came from.
- **Birds read by flight** — the eagle, the vulture, the kite. What matters is the
  line it flew and whether it climbed.
- A raven on the right is favourable and a crow on the right is not. Readers who
  have confused the two have got armies killed, and every reader can name one.

`[settled]` **The reader watches only inside the frame, whatever happens outside
it.** A thing can walk up behind him while he watches an empty rectangle of sky,
and he will not look.

### The birds that find land

`[settled]` **Where:** the Kurunatukas. Here the bird-reading and the navigation
are the same craft. A navigator watches for the frigatebird, which will not land on
water, and for the noddy and the tern, which fly out from land at dawn and back to
it at dusk — so the evening flight is a bearing. He reads the swell against the
hull with his body, lying down in the bottom of the canoe.

`[settled]` A Kurunatuka navigator asked whether the crossing will succeed and a
Mukka haruspex asked the same question are doing the same job. **The navigator is
right more often,** and the readers know it.

### The birds in a cage

`[settled]` **Where:** aboard ship, anywhere Mukka or Kuru sails.

Chickens are kept in a coop on deck and fed nothing until the question is asked.
Then grain is thrown down. **If they eat so greedily that the crumbs spill from
their beaks onto the deck, the answer is yes.** If they pick at it, the answer is
no. If they will not come out of the coop, the answer is do not.

`[settled]` The crews know how to get the answer they want, and so does every
officer, and the whole ship watches the feeding to see whether the birds were fed
yesterday. **A captain who has his chickens quietly starved the night before a raid
is cheating, and it cannot be proved.**

`[loose]` The story every sailor on Nila tells: an admiral was told the birds would
not eat, said they could drink instead, threw the coop over the rail, and lost every
ship he had that afternoon. Nobody agrees which admiral it was.

## 4. Bone and fire

`[settled]` **Where:** Kalikhat, and the deep cities. Underground there are no birds
and no sky, so the Underdark peoples read bone.

A sheep's shoulder blade is scraped clean, thinned, and held over coals until it
cracks. The reader turns it and reads the cracks: **how many, which way they run,
and whether any reaches an edge.** A crack that runs off the edge of the bone means
there is no answer, and that question does not get asked again that year.

`[settled]` The bones are **kept**. The question is cut into the bone beside the
crack, and the answer beside that, and the outcome added later when it is known.
Rooms of them.

`[loose]` 🔒 **This is where writing came from.** The scratched question beside the
crack is the oldest written thing on Nila, older than any inscription in Maalm, and
the drow and deep gnomes still cut their letters in the angular shapes a knife makes
on bone. `[OPEN]` whether anyone up top knows this.

## 5. Blood and the twigs

`[settled]` **Where:** Musleheim, and the orc tribes.

The beast is killed over a bowl. The blood is caught, and the reader dips a bundle
of twigs in it and **flings it** — over the posts, over the walls, over everyone
standing there. What is read is the spatter: where it lands, and on whom.

- **Blood on a man's face** is a claim on him. The question is his as well now.
- **Blood that does not reach the wall** is a refusal.

`[settled]` The meat is then boiled and eaten by everybody present, including the
question-asker, including guests. **Refusing the meat is refusing the answer, and it
is noticed.** This has killed people. A party that turns down a bowl in a Musleheim
longhouse has refused the answer in front of everyone in the hall.

`[settled]` **The great blót.** Every ninth year, at the grove: **nine of every male
creature — nine men, nine horses, nine dogs, nine hawks** — killed and hung in the
trees around the hall, and left. The bodies stay up. Bodies from earlier blóts are
still hanging there.

`[settled]` **The horses.** White horses are kept at public cost, never worked, never
ridden. For a question they are led out and walked across a line of spears laid on
the ground, and the priests walk beside them and **read the snorting and the
neighing**. The priests hold that they are the servants here and the horses are the
ones in the confidence of the gods. `[loose]` Nothing about these horses marks them
out to a stranger — no harness, no brand, no fence — and stealing or eating one is
the worst crime in Musleheim.

## 6. The pit

`[settled]` **Where:** Brauron, and anywhere with a Greek altar.

This one asks the dead directly. The procedure is given step by step below.

1. Dig a trench, a forearm square.
2. Pour in, in order: honey mixed with milk; then sweet wine; then water. Scatter
   white barley over all of it.
3. Cut the throats of a black ram and a black ewe so the blood runs down into the
   trench. Turn your own face away while it runs.
4. **The dead come to the blood.** All of them, in a crowd, out of the dark.
5. **Hold them off with a drawn sword.** They may not drink until you let them.
6. Let the one you came for drink. **Until it drinks it cannot speak, and it does
   not know you.** After it drinks, it speaks, and it tells the truth.

`[settled]` **At step five the party has to decide which of the dead may drink
first.** Whoever drinks, speaks — so the wrong ghost talks, and then the pit is used
up.

`[settled]` **The beast has to agree.** Greek altars sprinkle water on the victim's
head before the knife; the animal shakes its head, and the shake is counted as
consent. A beast that stands still and will not shake **cannot be sacrificed**, and
the reader will not be argued into it. `[loose]` A goat can refuse three times in a
row.

`[settled]` **Before a battle**, at the line, in the open: the seer cuts a goat's
throat and reads the flow, and if it is wrong he calls for another goat, and the
army stands there and takes it while he works through the herd. Men die during this.
Nobody advances until the flow is right. This is recorded practice. It can open a
session.

## 7. The smaller ones

`[settled]` Short methods, and things to have an NPC doing in the background.

- **The cockerel and the letters.** Grain is scattered over a ring of letters cut in
  the floor and a cockerel is set down in the middle. **The letters it pecks, in
  order, spell the answer.** It almost never spells a whole word. `[loose]` A ruler
  who did this and got the first four letters of a name had everyone in his kingdom
  with that name killed. Usable for the [Company](the-company.md) and for
  [Preem](../../../characters/preem.md).
- **The arrows.** At a fork in the road, with two roads and no way to choose: a
  bundle of marked arrows is shaken in a quiver until one falls out. Often done
  alongside a liver — the same man, at the same crossroads, doing three kinds of
  divination in a row because he does not like the first answer. See
  [handouts/ezekiel-21-belomancy.md](../../../handouts/ezekiel-21-belomancy.md).
- **The oak.** Priests who never wash their feet and sleep on the ground, reading the
  sound of the leaves in one enormous tree, and the doves in it. Questions are
  scratched on strips of lead and handed in. The strips are kept. The pile of them is
  thousands deep and most of the questions are small: *is the child mine, should I
  keep sheep, is she going to come back.* `[loose]` The strips can be read out at the
  table.
- **The fish.** Meat is thrown into a spring where the fish are fed and never taken.
  If they take it, yes. **If they push it away with their tails, the petitioner is
  told to leave the district.**
- **The lots.** A branch cut from a fruit tree, sliced into chips, each marked,
  scattered on white cloth. Three are picked up one at a time without looking. Norse.
  Easy to roll for.
- **The smoke.** Incense dropped on coals and the smoke read: which way it leans,
  whether it splits, whether it comes back down. Kuru's fire-wardens do this five
  times a day and write it down
  ([the Flame of Ankalia §5](../../kuru/lore/the-flame-of-ankalia.md)).
- **The barque.** Maalm's old practice, still done in the surviving temples: the
  god's image is carried out on poles on the shoulders of eight men, and the
  petitioner asks aloud. **The god lurches forward for yes and back for no.** Two
  ostraca are held up, one saying yes and one saying no, and the god's carriers dip
  toward one. No carrier will admit to steering. 🔒 In at least one temple in Maalm
  the carriers are not steering.
- **The entrails of a person.** `[loose]` 🔒 It is done. It is the fastest and the
  most detailed reading there is, and the people who use it say so plainly. The
  [Company](the-company.md) has a man who does it. Put it in the campaign the way the
  campaign puts in everything else — as ordinary business, done indoors, in front of
  witnesses.

## 8. The poem as the oracle

`[loose]` A table mechanic, and it costs nothing to run.

The campaign already treats poems as literally true
([myth & poem as literal truth](myth-and-poem-as-literal-truth.md)). So: at a shrine
that takes questions, **the answer is a line of verse taken at random.** The keeper
opens a book, or draws a strip from a jar, and reads out whatever is on it without
comment.

At the table this is done for real — open [`handouts/`](../../../handouts/) at
random, pick a line, and read it out flat.

## 9. What is happening to the omens now

`[loose]` 🔒 The campaign-level use of all of the above.

The methods in this file are **not supposed to agree.** They read different
questions off different beasts under different skies, and a Mukka liver and a
Musleheim blood-spatter have never been compared.

**They are starting to agree.** Over roughly the last year:

- Livers are coming out with the head-lobe absent, over and over, across
  unconnected readings.
- The birds come in from one quarter of sky no matter the question
  ([the Flame of Ankalia §6](../../kuru/lore/the-flame-of-ankalia.md)).
- The shoulder blades crack straight off the edge.
- The blood does not reach the wall.
- The chickens will not come out of the coop, on ships that have never met.

Every system is returning its own word for *the thing you are asking about is
already finished*, to people who cannot compare notes because they despise each
other's methods. **The comet is overhead and inbound
([overview §5](campaign-overview.md)), and nobody has put it together.** A party
that travels this widely can sit through readings in two regions and compare them.

`[OPEN]` Whether anyone else has noticed — a Mukka reader, a fire-warden, the
[Second Foundation](the-second-foundation.md).
