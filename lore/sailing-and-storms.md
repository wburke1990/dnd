# Sailing the Archipelago — crewing, weather, and storms

How the party gets from island to island. Nila is mostly water and the
campaign is a sandbox — the party sails wherever they want (see
[campaign-overview §5](campaign-overview.md)) — so the crossing itself is a
thing they *do*, not a cut to the next port. They crew the boat, the sea sets
the terms, and a storm is something they see coming and answer.

## The boat

The party sails Blackacre's boat — a commandeered sloop / sail-canoe he took
fleeing to Mukka (see [floating-fleet](floating-fleet.md) and
[characters/blackacre](../characters/blackacre.md)). It is small, fast, and
shallow: a lashed hull in the Fleet manner, quick and quiet, able to work close
inshore and up rivers where a dwarf-built galley cannot follow. Against a
boarding it is thin — its safety is speed and getting away, not standing to
fight (see [ships-and-sea-power](ships-and-sea-power.md)).

**Hull — the boat's condition.** The boat has **six hull boxes**. Storms,
rocks, and beasts strike them. While any remain, she sails. When the last is
struck she is taking water faster than the crew can throw it out: a **sinking
clock** starts — **three rounds** to beach her, patch the hole, or bail her
dry with magic before she goes down under whatever the crew can't carry.

**Repair.** A **day in a friendly port** clears every box. On the water, hand
repair clears **one box per hour** of work (STR/carpenter's tools, or a
Mending cantrip per box). Blackacre's magic does more, and this is the reason a
druid is worth a berth (see [floating-fleet](floating-fleet.md) — *"why a druid
is gold"*): **Shape Water** throws the bilge over the side, **Mending** closes
a seam, and once he can grow and shape timber he can replace a strake outright
rather than splint it. A boat with a druid aboard rarely sinks.

**Wear carries over.** Boxes struck on one leg and not repaired in port start
the next leg already gone. A neglected boat begins a crossing half-broken.

## Stations — everyone has a job

Five stations for the five PCs. Each names their station at the start of a leg
and works it each watch. This is the same footing as the boat-crossing in the
[dream quest's Apep gate](../encounters/the-dream-quest.md#gate-two--apep):
each round a player says what their character is physically doing, and rolls
the ability that fits it.

| Station | The work | Usual ability |
|---|---|---|
| **Helm** | steer; hold the boat's heading against sea and wind | DEX, or WIS to read the water |
| **Sails** | set, trim, and reef; get power out of the wind, or spill it | STR or DEX |
| **Lookout** | watch the sky, the water, and the horizon — weather, reefs, sails, and what's below | WIS (Perception) |
| **Navigator** | set and hold the course by star, current, and landmark | INT or WIS (Survival) |
| **Bosun** | keep her dry and whole — bail, mend, secure the gear | CON or STR (or druid magic) |

**Short-handed.** Fewer than five aboard, or a PC down, means stations go
unworked. A short crew raises every watch DC by **2**, and the read (below)
loses the extra eyes — take it at disadvantage. This is why crew, passengers,
and hired hands matter, and why the party may want more bodies than PCs.

## A voyage is a run of watches

- **Short hop** — a near neighbor in sight of departure: **one watch**.
- **Open crossing** — Maalm to the center, say, or Musleheim across the open
  sea ([nila-atlas](nila-atlas.md)): **three to five watches**.
- **The strait** and other named bad water: DM's count, and worse weather.

Each watch runs in three beats.

**1 · The read.** Lookout and Navigator each check against the watch DC.
- **Both succeed:** they see the watch clearly — the weather a beat before it
  lands, the reef before the hull finds it, whether they're holding course, and
  what shares the water with them. They choose how to meet it.
- **One succeeds:** they get part of it — the weather but not the position, or
  the course but not the sail on the horizon.
- **Both fail:** the sea acts first. The storm is on them, the rocks are under
  them, or the thing in the water has already risen before anyone calls it.

**2 · The work.** Helm, Sails, and Bosun check against the watch DC for what
their stations are doing that watch.

**3 · The tally.** Count the crew's successes for the watch:
- **Most of the crew succeeds:** a clean watch. Make way, hold course, nothing
  parts.
- **Half or fewer succeed:** a setback, scaled to how bad. Pick or roll: lose
  ground (**add a watch** to the leg), **strike one hull box**, drift off course
  (Navigator check next watch or fetch up somewhere unplanned — see *Getting
  lost*), or a line/spar carries away (a station works at disadvantage until
  the Bosun sets it right).

**Watch DC by weather:**

| Sea | DC | What it looks like |
|---|---:|---|
| **Calm** | 8 | slack water, light air; the boat wants working to move at all |
| **Fresh** | 10 | a working breeze; good sailing, nothing free |
| **Rising** | 13 | wind building, sea getting up, sky going wrong — the warning |
| **Gale** | 16 | hard weather short of a named storm; every watch costs |
| **Storm** | — | its own set-piece, below |

Set the weather, or roll **1d6** each watch and shift one step: **1–2** worse,
**3–4** the same, **5–6** better. Weather trends worse on open crossings, in
the strait, and in storm season; the DM leans the die.

## Weather, and how to avoid a storm

A storm does not fall out of a clear sky. **Rising** is the warning: the wind
builds, the swell lengthens, the sky goes hard and greenish. On a good read the
party sees it a watch out and chooses how to meet it, before it is on them.

- **Run before it.** Turn downwind and ride it out fast. Least dangerous to the
  hull, but it carries the boat well off course — Navigator check when it
  passes to learn where they've fetched up (often somewhere unplanned).
- **Heave to.** Hold position and take the beating to stay on course. Saves the
  leg, risks the hull — run the storm set-piece.
- **Make for shelter.** Race a lee shore or an island before the storm lands: a
  chase of Helm/Sails/Navigator checks against a rising DC. Reach it and they
  ride the storm at anchor in calm water. The island may be one no chart marks
  (see *Getting lost*).
- **Drive through.** Fastest to the far side, most dangerous — the storm at full
  strength with no downwind room to give. The set-piece at the hardest DC.

If the read failed and no one saw it coming, the party doesn't get the choice.
The storm set-piece opens with the crew already caught — surprised, stations
unset, and the first round at disadvantage.

## The storm set-piece

Run a storm the way the [Apep gate](../encounters/the-dream-quest.md#gate-two--apep)
runs: escalating rounds, everyone at a station, each round a player names what
their character is doing and rolls that station's ability against the **storm
DC** (start **15**; **17** for driving through; **+1 or +2** in the worst
water).

Three phases, at least a round each: **rising**, **peak**, **breaking**.

**Each round, the tally:**
- **Most of the crew succeeds:** they weather the round. Move to the next phase.
- **Half or fewer succeed:** trouble. Pick or roll:
  - **Strike a hull box** — a sea stoves in a strake, the mast strains, the
    steering fouls.
  - **Gear goes** — a sail blows out, a line parts, cargo or stores wash over
    (mark a stores box, below; a station works at disadvantage until reset).
  - **Overboard** — the sea takes whoever is most exposed toward the rail. That
    PC makes a **DC 13 STR (Athletics)** save or goes into the water. (See
    below — this is the real danger.)

**A PC overboard.** In a running sea a swimmer has moments. On their turn they
make **DC 13 STR (Athletics)** to reach a trailing line or the hull; another PC
can throw a line (an action, DC 12 DEX) or reach with a pole. Miss, and the
boat draws ahead — each round in the water is one **level of exhaustion** and
the range opens. Two rounds unreached and they are under; a **rescue** then
costs someone going in after on a line, or magic. A boat under sail does not
come about quickly.

**Blackacre and the weather.** Low-level, druid magic only helps at the
margins — Shape Water to bail, Mending to hold a seam, Create Water is no use
in a storm. This is the stretch of campaign where a gale can nearly sink them
and they have to sail their way out. Later it inverts: **Control Weather (8th)**
lays an ordinary storm flat, **Water Walk** and **Wind Walk** take the crew off
a foundering hull, and the weather stops being the threat (see
[fleet-warfare](fleet-warfare.md) — the same spells the Fleets fight with). Once
that is in reach, storms are no longer the danger; the *beasts* and the
*mage-winds* are (see *Scaling*).

**Breaking it.** Three clean rounds — one per phase — and the storm passes, or
a Control Weather working ends it outright. Then the reckoning: total the hull
boxes struck, and a Navigator check for where the storm has left them.

## Resting at sea

Standing watch is light activity by the rules, and a long rest allows up to two
hours of it on top of six hours' sleep — so keeping the boat watched and taking
a long rest are not opposed. Whether the *whole* party rests turns on how many
hands the night's weather needs at the stations.

**Easy nights — calm or fresh, one lookout enough.** Split the night into a
watch bill. With five aboard, each stands about a ninety-minute watch — inside
the two-hour allowance — and still gets six hours down. The whole party
completes a long rest and the boat is watched the whole night. The sixth berth
is slack: no one shares a bunk, and one passenger or hired hand rides along
without breaking this.

**Hard nights — rising, gale, or driving through.** Now the boat needs the helm
*and* the sails worked without a break — two or more hands up the whole time.
Cover that around the clock with only five and someone stands a real three- or
four-hour watch, past the two-hour allowance and short of six hours' sleep. On a
hard-sailing night the crew who keep her moving get **a short rest, not a long
one.** Rotate who stands the heavy watches so the loss moves around the party
night to night.

**Why fragmented sleep doesn't add up.** A long rest is one block — six
hours' sleep plus at most two hours of light activity, and only one such rest
per twenty-four hours — not a tally of naps banked across a day. Three two-hour
dozes between watch turns are three short sleeps, none of them the six-hour block
the rest needs, and stretching the night longer doesn't assemble one out of the
pieces. Broken sleep stays broken.

**Enough crew.** Hands enough to work the stations *and* free each PC for six
hours mean everyone rests even in a blow. Working two stations right through the
night and still resting all five PCs takes about eight aboard — so a hard passage
is the reason to ship extra crew, and the six-berth cap is the limit on how many
can rest at once. Short-handed on a long passage, the party wears down a person
at a time, a cost that stacks with the stores clock and the hull wear.

**Interrupted.** A storm that breaks in the night, or a beast, is an hour of
hard work or fighting — it ends the rest for everyone drawn into it, and the rest
starts over.

**At anchor or ashore.** Reach a sheltered anchorage or make landfall and the
sea does the keeping: the whole crew sleeps and takes a full long rest, at the
cost of the night's progress. The plainest way to rest hard is to stop.

## Getting lost — the sandbox delivers

A blown leg or a failed course is not a dead end. When the party runs before a
storm, fails navigation badly, or makes for the nearest shelter, they **fetch
up somewhere they did not aim for** — an island off the charts, a coast no one
aboard knows. Drop them at a prepared island, or roll for a strange landfall.
Bad sailing is how the party finds the water's stranger *places*; a clean
crossing skips those. (The [sea-beasts table](../bestiary/sea-beasts.md) is
separate — it's a **daily roll on any open-water leg**, wherever the boat is, so
the party can meet a monster on a clean crossing too; a botched leg just adds a
strange landfall on top.) Let the dice carry them off course and see where they
land.

## The cost — travel is not free

**Stores.** A crossing needs water and food. Give the boat a **stores track** —
one box per day for the crew, filled in port. Each watch-heavy day marks a box;
running dry starts daily **CON saves against exhaustion**. A leg blown off
course burns stores it didn't budget for, so a bad storm is also a supply
problem — the party may reach land hungry, or have to put in somewhere unplanned
to water and provision (another way onto an unmarked island).

**Wear.** Unrepaired hull boxes carry to the next leg (above). A boat that never
sees a friendly port grinds down.

**The water is hunted.** Trade here is anarchic and unescorted, and Mukka preys
on it ([ships-and-sea-power](ships-and-sea-power.md)); a slow, damaged, or
storm-scattered boat is the easy target. A gale that costs hull boxes also
leaves the party weaker for whatever finds them next — pirate, patrol, or beast.

## Scaling with the party

- **Now (~L3).** The weather and the beasts are the whole threat. A gale can
  strike four hull boxes and put someone overboard, and the crew has to sail its
  way clear. Keep DCs where they're written; a first bad storm should feel
  survivable but close.
- **Mid.** Blackacre's water and weather magic starts to bite. Ordinary storms
  become manageable; raise the stakes to named beasts (the sea-beasts table),
  Mukka in force, and foul water the magic doesn't cover.
- **High.** Control Weather, Water Walk, Wind Walk, and Transport via Plants
  make ordinary crossings routine (see [fleet-warfare](fleet-warfare.md)). The
  threats worth a scene are now the great primordials of the beast table, a
  rival fleet's **mage-wind** (a storm raised *against* them, which their own
  magic has to fight), and the set-piece fleet battles the late campaign builds
  toward.

## The world's memory of a storm

Before the party's first bad blow, let them hear what the sea has done. The
world remembers two things word-for-word (see
[ships-and-sea-power](ships-and-sea-power.md),
[saga-weather-omens](saga-weather-omens.md)): the **Suartleheim war-fleet that
broke the orc sea and then drowned to the last hull in a storm on the voyage
home**, and the **hail at Hjörungavágr** — the storm the earl bought with his
own son, every hailstone weighing its ounce. These are real saga lines, kept to
hand to the players as songs and tavern-tellings, not invented. A crew that has
heard them reads their own rising sea differently — and the mage-winds later in
the campaign land as those songs coming true (see
[fleet-warfare](fleet-warfare.md)).

## Quick DM checklist

1. **Leg length** — hop (1 watch) / crossing (3–5) / bad water (more).
2. **Stations** — each PC names one; short crew raises DCs by 2.
3. **Each watch** — the read (Lookout + Navigator), the work (Helm, Sails,
   Bosun), the tally. Set or roll the weather.
4. **Rising sea** — offer the choice: run, heave to, make for shelter, drive
   through.
5. **Storm** — the set-piece: rising / peak / breaking, tally each round, hull
   boxes and overboard on a bad round.
6. **Resting** — easy night: all hands long-rest on short watches. Hard-sailing
   night: those who crew her get a short rest only. Anchor or land to rest fully.
7. **Landfall** — repair hull in port, refill stores, and reckon where the
   storm actually left them.
