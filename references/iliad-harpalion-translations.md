# Iliad 13.650–655 — the death of Harpalion, across translations

*Real-world reference (not in-world canon). A translation comparison for the line we
want for blood consecration: Harpalion dies, and his blood wets the ground. Companion
to [the Iliad, blood on the ground](../handouts/iliad-blood-on-the-ground.md) and
[the rite at the Jotunheim trees](../world/lonka/encounters/jotunheim-trees-rite.md).*

**Why this one.** Of the blood-and-earth lines in the poem, this is the one where a
single named man's blood goes into the ground and the poem says so. The others
are battlefield-wide (4.451, 17.360) or belong to a chariot (20.499). Here it is one
man, and the blood goes into the ground.

**The Greek**, 13.654–655, checked against the Perseus text of the Oxford Classical
Text:

> θυμὸν ἀποπνείων, ὥς τε σκώληξ ἐπὶ γαίῃ
> κεῖτο ταθείς· ἐκ δ' αἷμα μέλαν ῥέε, δεῦε δὲ γαῖαν.

`δεῦε δὲ γαῖαν` — "and it wetted the earth." `δεύω` is the ordinary verb for wetting or
soaking a thing, the same word used of tears wetting a cheek. Homer uses no special
word here.

## The comparison

Line breaks: the verse translations quoted from scanned text (Lattimore, Fitzgerald,
Fagles, Mitchell, Alexander, Green, Wilson) come from full-text search of the scans,
which runs the lines together. Their wording is verbatim; their lineation is not shown.
Chapman, Cowper, Lombardo and Verity are quoted from sources that keep the line breaks,
marked with `/`.

| Translator (year) | The line | Note |
|---|---|---|
| George Chapman (1611) | *"it settled him, and set his spirit gone / Amongst the hands of his best friends; and like a worm he lay / Stretch’d on the earth, which his black blood imbru’d, and flow’d away."* | The blood does not wet the ground — it "imbru’d" the earth, then "flow’d away." Worm kept. Blood black. |
| Alexander Pope (1715–20) | *"Sunk in his sad companions' arms he lay, / And in short pantings sobb'd his soul away; / (Like some vile worm extended on the ground;) / While life's red torrent gush'd from out the wound."* | No verb — the ground is not mentioned; Pope keeps the blood on the wound and drops the earth. Worm kept, in parentheses, as "some vile worm." Blood red. |
| William Cowper (1791) | *"There ended his retreat; sudden he sank / And like a worm lay on the ground, his life / Exhaling in his fellow-warrior's arms, / And with his sable blood soaking the plain."* | "soaking," and what is soaked is the plain rather than the ground. Worm kept. Blood sable. |
| Lang, Leaf & Myers (1883) | *"And sitting down, even there, in the arms of his dear comrades, he breathed away his soul, lying stretched like a worm on the earth, and out flowed the black blood, and wetted the ground."* | "wetted the ground." Worm kept. Blood black. Closest to the Greek word order. |
| Samuel Butler (1898) | *"so he sat down where he was and breathed his last in the arms of his comrades, stretched like a worm upon the ground and watering the earth with the blood that flowed from his wound."* | "watering the earth." Worm kept. No colour — the blood is only "the blood that flowed from his wound." |
| A. T. Murray (1924) | *"And sitting down where he was in the arms of his dear comrades he breathed forth his life, and lay stretched out like a worm on the earth; and the black blood flowed forth and wetted the ground."* | "wetted the ground." Worm kept. Blood black. |
| E. V. Rieu (1950; quoted from the 1951 printing, which is what was scanned) | *"Harpalion collapsed forthwith, gasped out his life in the arms of his friends, and lay stretched on the ground like a worm, while the dark blood poured out of him and soaked the earth."* | "soaked the earth." Worm kept. Blood dark. |
| Richmond Lattimore (1951) | *"There, sitting among the arms of his beloved companions, he gasped out his life, then lay like a worm extended along the ground, and his dark blood drenched the ground in its running."* | "drenched the ground in its running." Worm kept. Blood dark. |
| Robert Fitzgerald (1974) | *"On the spot he sank down on his haunches, panting out his life amid the hands of fellow-soldiers: then he lengthened out like an earthworm as dark blood flowing from him stained the ground."* | "stained the ground." Earthworm. Blood dark. |
| Robert Fagles (1990) | *"He sank on the spot, hunched in his dear companion’s arms, gasping out his life as he writhed along the ground like an earthworm stretched out in death, blood pooling, soaking the earth dark red."* | "soaking the earth dark red." Earthworm. No colour on the blood; the earth is "dark red." |
| Stanley Lombardo (1997) | *"Harpalion sank into his comrades’ arms / And breathed out his life stretched on the ground / Like an earthworm in a pool of black blood."* | No verb — the ground is not wetted; the body lies in a pool. Earthworm. Blood black. |
| A. S. Kline (2009) | *"He collapsed on the spot, and sinking into the arms of his friends, breathed out his life and lay in the dust like a worm, the dark blood flowing and soaking the ground."* | "soaking the ground." Worm kept. Blood dark. |
| Anthony Verity (2011) | *"Harpalion collapsed on the spot and breathed out his spirit / in the arms of his companions, lying stretched out on the earth / like a worm; and the dark blood flowed out, and soaked the ground."* | "soaked the ground." Worm kept. Blood dark. |
| Stephen Mitchell (2011) | *"And sinking down on the spot, in the arms of his comrades he breathed out his life and lay there stretched on the earth like a dead worm; and the ground was soaked with his blood."* | "the ground was soaked with his blood" — the ground becomes the subject. A dead worm, and Mitchell's endnote explains why: a worm writhes when it is hurt and stretches out when it is dead. No colour. |
| Caroline Alexander (2015) | *"Sitting there, in the arms of his beloved companions, breathing his life forth, like a worm he lay outstretched upon the ground; and the dark blood flowed forth, and soaked the earth."* | "soaked the earth." Worm kept. Blood dark. |
| Peter Green (2015) | *"He sank, sat where he was, and in the arms of his comrades breathed out his life, and like a worm lay stretched there on the ground: his dark blood ran, soaked the earth."* | "soaked the earth." Worm kept. Blood dark. |
| Emily Wilson (2023) | *"He slumped there, seated, and his loving comrades embraced him in their arms as he was breathing his life out. Then he lay down on the ground, stretched out, wormlike, and black blood poured from him and drenched the earth."* | "drenched the earth." The worm becomes an adjective, "wormlike" — there is no worm lying there, only the comparison. Blood black. |

## Where the versions differ

The versions differ in three places:

- **`δεῦε`** — wetted, soaked, drenched, watered. Lang-Leaf-Myers and Murray say
  "wetted"; Butler says "watering"; Rieu, Kline, Verity, Alexander and Green say
  "soaked"; Lattimore and Wilson say "drenched"; Fitzgerald says "stained." Chapman has
  the earth "imbru’d" and the blood flowing away. Pope and Lombardo drop the verb: Pope
  keeps the blood at the wound, Lombardo puts the body in a pool.
- **the worm** — *"stretched out like a worm"* is in the Greek and every translator here
  keeps it in some form. Pope makes it "some vile worm." Fitzgerald,
  Fagles and Lombardo make it an earthworm. Mitchell makes it a dead worm and explains
  the point in a note. Wilson turns it into the adjective "wormlike."
- **`αἷμα μέλαν`** — black blood, or dark blood. The Greek says black. Chapman,
  Lang-Leaf-Myers, Murray, Lombardo and Wilson say black; Pope says red; Cowper says
  sable; Rieu, Lattimore, Fitzgerald, Kline, Verity, Alexander and Green say dark.
  Butler and Mitchell give it no colour, and Fagles moves the colour onto the earth.
